# Manuscript and prose

Use this reference for planning, substantive editing, abstracts, and summaries. Scale it to the supplied section; a paragraph edit does not require a whole-paper reconstruction.

## Build the argument

Write the contribution in one defensible sentence. Identify the question, the gap that makes it consequential, the evidence that addresses it, and the scope of the resulting conclusion. Map the key results to that contribution before reorganizing the paper.

Use the venue's structure. In a conventional research article:

- Introduction: establish the problem and relevant prior work, specify the unresolved question, and explain what this study addresses. A method's popularity is not a scientific gap.
- Methods: describe what was actually done in sufficient detail to assess and reproduce the analysis. Ask about missing details rather than reconstructing them from customary practice.
- Results: give the question, observation or estimate, uncertainty, and bounded interpretation. Choose an order that makes the argument assessable; avoid copying the chronology of the laboratory notebook.
- Discussion: explain what follows from the results, compare with verified prior evidence, test alternative explanations, and identify the limits and next discriminating step.

Keep a clear route between the abstract, central claims, and evidence. Do not force IMRAD, a novelty claim, or a fixed number of paragraphs when the genre uses another structure.

## Polish without changing the science

Give each paragraph a specific job. Put essential context before an unfamiliar inference, keep the subject and verb easy to find, and use transitions that name the logical relationship. Prefer concrete mechanisms and comparisons to promotional adjectives.

Preserve technical terms when synonyms could change meaning. Do not replace every repeated term for stylistic variety. Preserve necessary hedging and distinguish evidence strength from an author's confidence. Improve passive constructions when agency matters; retain them when the procedure or object is the appropriate focus.

Reduce filler, repeated framing, invented contrasts, generic importance statements, and inflated claims. Do not apply word bans, punctuation bans, a universal first-person voice, or supposed AI-detector optimization. Match the author's effective voice and the target genre.

For example, “The design revolutionizes assembly” needs a defined contribution and evidence. “The design increased the measured assembly yield under [reported conditions]” is usable only if those measurements exist. Do not invent the yield or conditions to make the sentence concrete.

## Abstracts and public summaries

Include enough context to understand the question, the method at the right level, the main supported result, and its implication. Preserve a prospective tense for proposed work. Explain specialized terms for a public audience without turning a plausible application into a demonstrated device, clinical result, or societal benefit.

For strict limits, count the final delivered string, including spaces when required. Clarify how headings, equations, citations, or markup are counted if the application rules distinguish them. A source-file count alone does not establish the count in a submission form.

## Source and rendering check

Check that proposed edits preserve citation scope, mathematical statements, symbols, and units. Compile LaTeX after file changes using the project's established command, and inspect the relevant PDF pages when layout matters. A compile success does not establish that a figure fits or that the document meets a page limit.

## Further reading

The procedures here also reflect the original local academic-writing skill; they are not a transcription of that article.
