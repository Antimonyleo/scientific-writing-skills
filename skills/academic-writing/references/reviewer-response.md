# Reviewer responses and revision verification

Use for journal reviews, grant-panel questions, or revision correspondence. The editor's or sponsor's instructions determine the required format. Author revision assistance does not imply an external review assignment or require a new permission ceremony.

## Track every concern

Use stable identifiers such as R1.2a when one numbered comment contains several requests. Preserve the reviewer's meaning; retain exact wording when preparing a point-by-point response from user-supplied comments. Separate the request from your interpretation of the underlying concern.

For multi-comment work, maintain:

| ID and concern | Evidence or decision needed | Response | Document change and location | State |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Use states that reflect reality: unresolved, proposed, implemented, verified, or declined with reason. A drafted response is not an implemented revision. Cross-reference overlapping concerns while answering each reviewer directly.

## Resolve the science before promising a change

Classify the action: clarification; report existing evidence; reanalysis; new experiment or simulation; narrowed claim; or reasoned disagreement. Address concerns affecting the central conclusion first. For a missing result, draft conditional wording or a marked placeholder, never a completed experiment or favorable result.

For conflicting reviewer requests, identify the scientific or editorial tradeoff and propose a coherent solution. A polite reply cannot resolve an unaddressed validity problem. If a requested study lies outside the supported scope, explain how the current evidence supports the bounded claim and what limitation remains. When necessary, identify the question for the editor or program officer; do not contact them without authorization.

## Draft a direct response

Answer the specific concern, explain the evidence or reasoning, and identify the relevant change. Keep the tone respectful and specific. Acknowledge valid criticism without formulaic gratitude for every sentence or unnecessary self-blame. Include enough revised wording to make the change clear and cite its actual location when available. Do not invent page or line numbers.

Keep the response separate from the proposed manuscript or proposal change. Clearly distinguish text already present from newly added text. Support a disagreement with evidence and an explicit rationale rather than deference, dismissal, or an unsupported claim of consensus.

An unperformed analysis can be described internally as “Proposed response, pending analysis.” It must not appear in a submission-ready letter as “We performed the analysis.” If the user requests only a draft response, mark any dependent changes as proposed and complete the draft.

## Verify against the actual revision

Compare the response with the revised source, figures, tables, supplement, and resulting output that are available. Check that every promised analysis exists, each quoted revision matches, related claims are consistent, and the locators refer to the stated version. Refresh page and line references after the final render.

Only mark a concern verified after checking the relevant revision and support. If a promised change cannot be checked in the supplied revision and supporting evidence, retain proposed or unresolved status. Verification does not require that the assistant made the edit. An excerpt permits verification of that excerpt, not of a change throughout an unavailable manuscript. A completed response table establishes comment coverage, not scientific validity or acceptance.

## Further reading

Noble, [Ten simple rules for writing a response to reviewers](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005730), informs complete, direct, navigable responses. State tracking and verification here are workflow design choices, not requirements imposed by that article.
